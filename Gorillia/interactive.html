Why Palette Choice Matters

The same jellyfish viewed through different FLIR palettes would look completely different — but contain identical temperature data. Rainbow palette would show it transitioning through every color. Ironbow would render its warm interior organs as glowing metal. Arctic would show it as a bright shape against cold dark water.

This is the central lesson of the FLIR palette system: the palette is a design decision. You are choosing how to represent data, not which data to show. Every palette reveals the same truth — but emphasizes different aspects of it.

FLIR Palette System

5 palettes — 5 different stories from the same data

🌈 Rainbow

The FLIR classic — runs the full visible spectrum from cold violet-black through every color to hot white. Maximum color differentiation across the full temperature range. Best for: scientific analysis where you need to see every temperature variation simultaneously. The most information-dense palette available.

🔥 Ironbow

Simulates the visual appearance of heated metal — from cold black through dark red, orange, yellow to white-hot. Intuitive reading because it mirrors how we see real heat in everyday objects. Best for: identifying hot spots in mechanical or electrical equipment, or for dramatic visual presentation of biological thermal signatures.

⬜ White Hot

Grayscale with hot surfaces appearing bright white and cold surfaces dark. Maximum spatial detail and sharpness. Best for: identifying shapes, textures, and fine detail in thermal scenes. The most common palette for security, surveillance, and search-and-rescue applications because detail takes priority over temperature discrimination.

⬛ Black Hot

The inverse of White Hot — hot surfaces appear dark, cold surfaces bright. Useful when you need to find cool objects against warm backgrounds. Best for: detecting cool anomalies (leaks, missing insulation, cold spots) in otherwise warm industrial environments. Preferred by some operators for reduced eye fatigue in extended observation sessions.

🔵 Arctic / Cool

Inverts the temperature emphasis — cold reads bright (white/cyan), hot reads dark. Used to find cold anomalies rather than heat sources. Best for: detecting insulation failures (cold bridges), finding cold air infiltration in building diagnostics, or imaging cold-blooded animals against warm environments where standard palettes would struggle to differentiate.

🎯 Hot Target Highlight

Displays the scene in near-grayscale but isolates only the hottest zones in high-contrast yellow-white highlight. Everything else falls to dark neutral tones. Best for: predator detection in hunting applications, finding injured animals in field research, or any situation where you need to instantly identify the single hottest object regardless of scene complexity.
